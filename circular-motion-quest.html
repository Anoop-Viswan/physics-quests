Stage 1 · Hook · 5 min

The roller coaster that broke necks

In 1895, Coney Island in New York opened the Flip Flap Railway, one of America's first upside-down roller coasters. Its loop was a perfect circle. Riders lined up for blocks.

Then the complaints started: sore necks, whiplash, people staggering off dizzy. The loop was small, about 25 feet across, and to make it safely over the top, the car had to enter the bottom very fast. Down at the bottom of that tight circle, riders were crushed into their seats with forces reported at up to 12 times their weight. The ride closed in 1903.

For decades, nobody built a loop again. Then in 1976 a coaster called Revolution opened in California with a loop that wasn't a circle: tall and narrow, shaped like an upside-down teardrop. Nearly every looping coaster since has used that shape.

Why does the shape of a loop change how hard it pushes on you? Toggle the two loops and keep that question in your head.

The Moon is falling

Right now, the Moon is accelerating toward Earth at about 0.0027 m/s². It never hits us because it's moving sideways fast enough to keep "missing". Newton used exactly this number to prove that apple-gravity and Moon-gravity are the same force. That's the boss question in Stage 5.

Pilots and 9 g

In a tight turn, a fighter pilot's seat has to push up to 9 times their weight to bend their path into a circle. Their blood "lags" toward their feet, and without a special suit they black out.

Stage 3 · Learn · 35–45 min

The ideas, one at a time

A. Moving in a circle means accelerating Topic 2.9

ac = v² / r  ·  v = 2πr / T
  • Velocity is tangent to the circle. Even at constant speed, its direction keeps changing, so there is an acceleration.
  • That acceleration points toward the center ("centripetal" means "center-seeking").
  • Double the speed → 4 times the acceleration. Half the radius → twice the acceleration (at the same speed).
  • If the inward force vanishes (the string breaks), the object flies off in a straight line along the tangent (1st law), not outward along the radius.

B. The recipe: there is no "centripetal force" arrow

  1. FBD with only real forces (gravity, normal, tension, friction).
  2. Choose + as toward the center.
  3. ΣFtoward center = mv²/r. Forces pointing in count +, and forces pointing out count −.
  4. Solve. "Centripetal force" is just the name for whichever real force (or combination) points inward: tension for a ball on a string, friction for a car on a flat curve, gravity for an orbit, normal plus gravity in a loop.

The outward "push" you feel in a turning car isn't a force. It's your body trying to keep going straight (inertia) while the car turns under you.

D. Cars on curves

  • Flat curve: static friction points sideways toward the center (the tires aren't skidding). μsmg = mv²max/r → vmax = √(μsgr). The mass cancels.
  • Hilltop (center below you): mg − FN = mv²/r. You feel lighter, and at v = √(gr) you get "airtime".
  • Bottom of a dip (center above you): FN − mg = mv²/r. You feel heavier.
  • Banked curve: part of the normal force points toward the center, so even with no friction there's one speed that works.

E. Orbits 2.6 + 2.9

GMm/r² = mv²/r → v = √(GM/r)
  • Gravity is the only force, and it IS the centripetal force. The satellite's mass cancels.
  • Farther orbits are slower, and they have longer periods: T² ∝ r³ (Kepler's 3rd law).
  • An orbiting astronaut is in free fall, so their apparent weight is 0, while gravity is still about 90% of surface strength in low orbit.

Case closed: why loops became teardrops

At the top of a loop, the minimum safe speed is v = √(gr). A circular loop has a big radius at the top too, so the car needs a high speed there, and so an even higher speed entering the bottom. At the bottom, FN = mg + mv²/r: a high speed in a tight circle means a huge normal force, and that's the crushing feeling.

The teardrop (clothoid) loop has a small radius at the top, so a slower car still makes it over, since v²/r stays big enough. It has a large radius at the bottom, so the same speed gives a much smaller v²/r, and a gentler push into the seat. Same thrill at the top, without the whiplash at the bottom.

Circular-motion points

Same question, two answers

A ball on a string moves in a vertical circle at constant speed. Is the tension greater at the top or the bottom? Justify.

0 / 3

"The bottom, because the centrifugal force pulls it down there."

Uses a fictitious force and never names Newton's 2nd law.

3 / 3

"The bottom. The net force toward the center has to be mv²/r at both points, and that's the same size since the speed is constant. At the top, gravity points toward the center, so T = mv²/r − mg. At the bottom, gravity points away from the center, so T = mv²/r + mg. So the tension at the bottom is larger, by 2mg."

The 4-line derive for any circular problem

  1. "Taking toward the center as positive, ΣF = mac = mv²/r."
  2. List the real forces with signs (e.g. FN − mg).
  3. Set them equal and solve in symbols.
  4. Check a limit (v → 0? r → ∞?) and make sure the sign makes sense.

The 7 circular-motion traps

  • Drawing a "centripetal force" or "centrifugal force" arrow on an FBD.
  • Saying a = 0 because the speed is constant. The direction changes!
  • An object released from a circle flying outward. It goes along the tangent.
  • Tension pointing up at the top of a vertical circle. A string always pulls toward the center.
  • Mixing up the top and bottom formulas.
  • "Doubling v doubles the force." It quadruples it (v²).
  • Using g = 9.8 for an orbit far from Earth. Use GM/r².
