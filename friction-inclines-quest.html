Stage 1 · Hook · 5 min

The book that two cars couldn't pull apart

Maya bet her brother Leo $5 he couldn't pull apart two notebooks. Leo laughed. Then he watched her interleave them: page 1 of book A, then page 1 of book B, then page 2 of A, and so on, like shuffling a deck of cards. No glue, no tape.

Leo grabbed both spines and pulled. Nothing. He braced his feet on the table and pulled harder. The harder he pulled, the tighter the books seemed to grip. He paid the $5.

This isn't a party trick with a secret. TV's MythBusters tried it with two interleaved phone books. Strongmen failed. Two cars failed. In the end it took two military vehicles pulling in opposite directions to rip them apart.

Each page is light and smooth. So where does a force big enough to beat two cars come from?

Friction is why you can walk

When you step, your shoe pushes BACK on the ground, and static friction pushes you FORWARD. On perfectly frictionless ice you couldn't take a single step forward.

Why anti-lock brakes win

A skidding tire uses kinetic friction. A rolling tire grips with static friction, which can be bigger. ABS stops the wheels from locking so the car can use the stronger static friction and stop in a shorter distance.

Stage 3 · Learn · 35–45 min

The ideas, one at a time

A. Static vs. kinetic friction Topic 2.7

fs ≤ μsFN  ·  fk = μkFN
  • Static (surfaces not slipping): friction is whatever it needs to be to stop slipping, up to a maximum of μsFN. Push with 10 N and it pushes back with 10 N. Push with 20 N and it pushes back with 20 N.
  • Kinetic (sliding): friction is fixed at μkFN, however fast it slides or however hard you push.
  • Usually μk < μs. It's harder to start something sliding than to keep it sliding.
  • Friction always opposes slipping between the two surfaces. That's not always the same as opposing the motion: a crate on an accelerating truck is dragged FORWARD by friction.
  • Friction doesn't depend on contact area. A brick on its side or flat gives the same friction.

B. The normal force is NOT always mg

FN is whatever the surface needs to push so the object doesn't sink into it. Find it from ΣF = 0 (or ma) perpendicular to the surface, every time.

C. Inclines: tilt your axes

along: mg sin θ  ·  perpendicular: mg cos θ
  1. Draw the FBD: mg straight DOWN, FN perpendicular to the ramp, friction along the ramp (opposite the slipping).
  2. Tilt the axes: x along the ramp and y perpendicular. Now only gravity needs splitting.
  3. The angle between mg and the y-axis equals the ramp angle θ. (Sanity check: at θ = 0, all of mg is "perpendicular", so cos 0 = 1 ✓.)
  4. y: FN = mg cos θ. x: ΣF = mg sin θ − f = ma.

D. Friction that makes things GO

  • Walking / running: your foot pushes back on the ground, so static friction on you points forward.
  • Car speeding up: the drive wheels push back on the road, so the road's static friction pushes the car forward. That's the external force on the car.
  • Crate on an accelerating truck: static friction points forward, the direction of the crate's acceleration. Its maximum gives amax = μsg.
  • Car on a flat curve: static friction points sideways, toward the center (see Circular Motion Quest).

Case closed: the unbreakable books

Each page touches its neighbours on both sides, and every contact is a friction surface. Hundreds of pages give hundreds of friction forces, all pointing against the pull. On top of that, the pages fan out from the spines at a slight angle, so pulling on the spines squeezes the pages together. That increases the normal force on every page, and so the maximum static friction too (f ≤ μsFN). The harder you pull, the harder it grips. That's why Leo felt the books "fight back".

Ramp and friction points

The ramp derive routine (works every time)

  1. FBD with FN perpendicular to the ramp. Friction points opposite the slipping (if it's at rest, opposite the way it would slip).
  2. Perpendicular: FN = mg cos θ (plus or minus any applied force components).
  3. Along: ΣF = ma with + in the direction of motion.
  4. Substitute f = μkFN (sliding) or f = μsFN ("about to slip", "maximum angle", "minimum force").
  5. Simplify. m usually cancels: a = g(sin θ − μkcos θ). Then check limits: μ = 0 gives a = g sin θ ✓, and θ = 90° gives a = g ✓.

Static friction wording that earns the point

0 / 1

"Friction = μsmg = 0.5 × 5 × 9.8 = 24.5 N" (for a box that isn't moving, pushed with 20 N).

Treats the maximum as the actual value.

1 / 1

"The box is at rest, so ΣF = 0 and static friction equals the push, 20 N. This is less than the maximum μsFN = 24.5 N, so the box stays at rest."

Uses equilibrium for the value and μsFN only as the check.

Only use μsFN as the friction value when the question says "just about to slip", "maximum", "minimum force to start", or "steepest angle".

Same question, two answers

A crate rides in a truck that is speeding up. Which way does friction on the crate point? Justify.

0 / 2

"Backward, because friction opposes motion."

2 / 2

"Forward. The crate accelerates forward with the truck, so by Newton's 2nd law the net force on it points forward. Static friction from the bed is the only horizontal force on the crate, so it must point forward."

The 7 friction & ramp traps

  • Using μsFN when the object is at rest and not about to slip.
  • FN = mg on a ramp, or with an angled rope. Re-derive it every time.
  • sin and cos swapped. Check it with θ = 0.
  • Drawing FN straight up on a ramp.
  • Adding a separate "down-the-ramp force". It's just mg sin θ, which is part of gravity.
  • "Friction always opposes motion." It opposes slipping.
  • Thinking heavier blocks slide faster down the same ramp. The mass cancels.
