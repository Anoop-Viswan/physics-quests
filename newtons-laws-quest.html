Stage 1 · Hook · 5 min

The horse who read Newton

Barnaby was a cart horse with a library card. One night he read Newton's third law: for every force, there is an equal and opposite force.

Next morning the farmer hitched him to a cart full of pumpkins. Barnaby didn't move.

It's pointless, he said. Whatever force I put on the cart, the cart puts the same force back on me, in the opposite direction. Equal and opposite forces cancel out. Physics says this cart can never move. I'm going back to bed.

The farmer had no physics degree, so he couldn't argue. Barnaby went back to bed.

Was Barnaby right? Carts obviously move, so something in his argument is wrong. By the end of Stage 4 you'll be able to say exactly what.

These are the two forces Barnaby is talking about. They really are equal and opposite. So why does the cart move anyway?

You move the Earth

Every time you jump, you push Earth away with the same force it pushes you up. Earth recoils by less than a hundred-millionth of a proton's width. Same force, enormous mass, tiny acceleration.

A truck and a mosquito

When a mosquito hits a truck's windshield, which one feels the bigger force? Most adults get this wrong. You'll answer it in Stage 2.

A. Forces and free-body diagrams Topic 2.2

A force is a push or pull from one object on another object. If you can't name the object doing the pushing, the force doesn't exist.

A book at rest on a table. Only two objects touch it or pull on it: Earth (gravity) and the table (normal force). Two forces, two arrows, and nothing else.

  • Draw the object as a dot. Every arrow starts on the dot and points away from it.
  • Label every arrow: Fg, FN, Ff, FT, Fapp.
  • Arrow lengths show relative size. Forces that balance get equal lengths.
  • No components, no velocity arrows, and no "ma" arrow on the diagram.
  • Phantom forces lose the point: "force of motion", "force of inertia", "the force from the throw" after the ball leaves your hand, and an extra "centripetal force" arrow.

B. Newton's first law Topic 2.4

ΣF = 0 ⇔ constant velocity

If the net force is zero, the velocity doesn't change. That includes v = 0 (at rest), and it also includes cruising at 30 m/s. Moving doesn't need a force. Changing velocity does. This is called translational equilibrium.

C. Newton's second law Topic 2.5

a = ΣF / m
  • Acceleration points the same way as the net force, not necessarily the same way as the velocity.
  • Recipe: FBD → pick + direction (the way it accelerates) → ΣF = ma along that axis → solve in symbols → substitute numbers.
  • Elevator, accelerating up at a: FN − mg = ma, so FN = m(g + a). The scale reads FN, so it reads more than your weight.
  • Several blocks moving together: treat them as one system to find a, then use one block alone to find the force between them.

D. Newton's third law Topic 2.3

FA on B = −FB on A

Third-law pairs act on different objects, so they never appear on the same FBD and never cancel each other. Balanced forces act on the same object. Mixing these two up is the most common Unit 2 mistake.

3rd-law pairBalanced forces (1st law)
Acts onTwo different objectsThe same object
Force typeAlways the same type (gravity ↔ gravity)Can be different types
Always equal?Yes, always, even during a crashOnly when a = 0
Book on tableEarth pulls book ↔ book pulls EarthEarth's gravity on book ↔ table's normal force on book

Test for a pair: swap the two object names. "Earth pulls book" becomes "book pulls Earth". If the swapped sentence is the other force, they're a pair.

How to collect every point

FRQs are graded line by line against a rubric. Each point needs specific words or a specific step. The physics can be right and still score zero if the rubric's words aren't there.

The question verbs

The question saysTo earn the point
Draw and label the forcesDot, arrows starting on the dot, one per real force, labelled, with sensible relative lengths. No components, no phantom forces.
DeriveStart from a law in symbols (ΣF = ma), substitute the FBD forces, then solve. Final answer only uses the variables the question lists.
CalculateWrite the equation, show the numbers going in, give the answer with units.
Justify / ExplainClaim + law + link (below). One-word answers score 0.
Sketch a graphLabel both axes, get the shape right (line or curve? up or down?), and show where it crosses the axis.
Is this consistent with…Name the earlier part and use its equation or result in your answer.

Claim → Law → Link: the justify template

ClaimAnswer the question directly. "The scale reading is greater than mg."
LawName the physics. "Newton's 2nd law: FN − mg = ma."
LinkApply it to this situation. "The acceleration is upward, so FN = m(g + a) > mg."

Same question, two answers

A person stands on a scale in an elevator that is speeding up as it moves upward. Is the scale reading greater than, less than, or equal to the person's weight? Justify your answer.

0 / 2

"Greater, because the elevator is going up."

Points to velocity ("going up") instead of acceleration, and names no law. An elevator going up at constant speed would read equal, so this reasoning is wrong even with the right letter.

2 / 2

"Greater. The only forces on the person are FN up from the scale and Fg = mg down. By Newton's 2nd law, FN − mg = ma. The acceleration is upward, so FN = m(g + a), which is greater than mg. The scale reads FN."

Claim, FBD forces, law, and the direction of a, linked to the reading.

Typical 2-point rubric: 1 point for relating the reading to an upward net force / upward acceleration, 1 point for using Newton's 2nd law with FN, mg and a. (A sample rubric for practice, not an official College Board one.)

The derive routine

Example: blocks of mass m₁ and m₂ touch on a frictionless floor. Force F pushes m₁. Derive the force m₁ exerts on m₂.

  1. Choose the system. For a, use both blocks: F = (m₁ + m₂)a, so a = F / (m₁ + m₂).
  2. Isolate. FBD of m₂ alone. The only horizontal force on it is F12.
  3. Apply the law in symbols. F12 = m₂a.
  4. Substitute and box it. F12 = m₂F / (m₁ + m₂).
  5. Check a limit. If m₂ → 0, F12 → 0. That makes sense, since you can't push hard on something with no mass. Graders like seeing this check in "explain" parts.

Test-day rules

  1. Read every FRQ first (1 min). Start with the one you're most sure of.
  2. Draw an FBD for every dynamics part, even when the question doesn't ask for one. It shows you the equation.
  3. Write symbols first and numbers last. Most points are in the setup, not the arithmetic.
  4. Never leave a part blank. Points are given line by line, so a correct first step still scores.
  5. If part (b) uses your answer to part (a) and (a) was wrong, still use it. Graders usually don't take the point away twice.
  6. Units on every final number. Use g = 9.8 m/s² unless told otherwise.
  7. With 3 minutes left, stop polishing and write a claim plus a law for every unanswered "justify".

The 8 Unit 2 traps

  • Moving ≠ net force. Constant velocity means ΣF = 0.
  • Normal force ≠ mg on inclines, in elevators, or when a rope pulls at an angle.
  • FN and Fg on a book are not a 3rd-law pair.
  • 3rd-law forces are equal even when the masses aren't. Truck = mosquito.
  • At the top of a throw, v = 0 but a = g. The net force is mg, downward.
  • Mass (kg) and weight (N) are different things. Weight = mg.
  • Static friction is "up to" μsFN, not always equal to it.
  • No "centripetal force" arrow on an FBD. Centripetal is a direction, not an extra force.
