Stage 1 · Hook · 5 min

The jumper who cheated gravity (legally)

In 1968, a 21-year-old engineering student named Dick Fosbury walked into the Olympic high-jump final in Mexico City with a technique everyone thought was a joke. Every other jumper went over the bar face-down, scissoring their legs. Fosbury ran in on a curve, turned his back to the bar, and went over backwards, head first, his body arched like a bridge.

The crowd laughed. Then he won the gold medal with an Olympic record of 2.24 m.

Today almost every elite high jumper uses the "Fosbury Flop". Here's the strange part: done perfectly, the jumper's body goes OVER the bar while their center of mass goes UNDER it.

How can a body clear a bar that its own "middle" never rises above? Toggle the picture and keep that question in your head.

You weigh less in Ecuador

g is about 9.78 N/kg at the equator and 9.83 N/kg at the poles. Same you, same mass, but a bathroom scale reads about 0.5% less near the equator. Earth's bulge and spin are why.

Gravity in orbit: still 89%

The Space Station is only 400 km up. Gravity there is about 8.7 N/kg, almost full strength. Astronauts float for a different reason. You'll predict it in Stage 2.

A. Systems: draw a box around what you care about Topic 2.1

A system is whatever you choose to draw a dashed box around: one block, two blocks and a string, a horse and a cart, or Earth and the Moon. That choice decides which forces count.

  • Internal forces act between objects inside the box. They come in 3rd-law pairs and cancel, so they can't accelerate the system as a whole.
  • External forces come from outside the box. Only these change the motion of the system's center of mass: ΣFext = Mtotal·acm.
  • If the inside details don't matter, you can treat the whole system as one object sitting at its center of mass. That's why you can draw a whole car as a dot on an FBD.

Recall lesson 1: horse + cart as one system. The rope forces were inside the box, so they cancelled, and only the ground's push on the hooves moved the whole thing.

B. Center of mass Topic 2.1

xcm = Σ mixi / Σ mi

The center of mass is a mass-weighted average position. Heavier objects pull it toward themselves. For symmetric objects (a uniform rod, ball or ring), it sits on the lines of symmetry, so it's at the geometric center. That point can even be in empty space: the center of a ring or a donut, or under the bar for a Fosbury jumper.

Try this: (1) Make the masses equal. Where is the center of mass? (2) Make B 3 times A. What fraction of the way from A to B is the center of mass? (3) Can you put the center of mass outside the region between the two masses? (No. Why not?)

C. Universal gravitation Topic 2.6

Fg = G m₁m₂ / r²
  • Every pair of masses attracts, with equal and opposite forces (3rd law!). The apple pulls Earth exactly as hard as Earth pulls the apple.
  • r is measured center to center, not from the surface. Altitude h above Earth means r = RE + h.
  • Inverse square: double r and the force drops to ¼. Triple it and it drops to 1/9. It never reaches zero.
  • G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². That's tiny, which is why you don't feel your phone pulling you.

D. Gravitational field, weight, and "apparent weight" Topic 2.6

g = G M / r²  (N/kg = m/s²)
  • g is a property of the place, not of the object: the gravitational force per kilogram. Near Earth's surface, g = 9.8 N/kg.
  • Weight Fg = mg changes with location. Mass doesn't change.
  • Apparent weight = what a scale reads = the normal force. It changes when you accelerate (elevators), and it's zero in free fall, even though gravity is still acting.
  • Mass shows up twice: as "gravitational mass" (how hard gravity pulls) and as "inertial mass" (how hard you are to accelerate). They're equal, so in free fall a = mg/m = g for every object. Hammer and feather land together.

Case closed: how the flop beats the bar

The center of mass is a weighted average of where all the body's mass is. When the body is arched over the bar, the head and arms hang down on one side, the legs on the other, and the hips sit on top. Average all of that and the result lands in the empty space below the arch, under the bar. Like the center of a ring, it doesn't have to be inside the body.

Why does it matter? The run-up energy has to lift the center of mass (that's what gravity "sees"). A jumper whose center of mass rises less can clear a higher bar with the same legs. Fosbury found a loophole in where "the middle" of a body can be.

Stage 6 · FRQ playbook · read twice before the test

Turning gravity into points

Factor-of-change: the 3-line ratio method

"Planet X has 3 times Earth's mass and 2 times its radius. Find gX." Don't guess. Write the ratio:

  1. Symbols: g = GM/R².
  2. Ratio: gX/gE = (MX/ME) · (RE/RX)² = 3 · (1/2)² = 3/4.
  3. Answer: gX = ¾ × 9.8 = 7.4 N/kg.

This handles every "if mass doubles and distance triples…" question, in multiple choice and in QQT parts. Remember to flip the distance ratio, because r is on the bottom.

Same question, two answers

An astronaut on the Space Station floats. A student says "there's no gravity in orbit." Do you agree? Justify.

0 / 3

"Disagree, there is gravity in space."

Correct claim, but no law, no number, and it never explains why she floats.

3 / 3

"Disagree. By F = GMm/r², gravity at r = 6800 km is (6400/6800)² ≈ 0.89 of its surface value, so it's still strong. She floats because she and the station both fall toward Earth with the same acceleration, so the floor doesn't need to push on her: the normal force (her apparent weight) is zero."

Claim, law with a number, and a link to the real reason (FN = 0 in free fall).

Center of mass in FRQs

  • Symmetric object? Say "by symmetry, the center of mass is at the geometric center". That sentence alone can earn the point.
  • Several pieces? Write xcm = Σmx/Σm with the numbers substituted, and include the total mass in the denominator.
  • "No external horizontal force" → the system's center of mass doesn't accelerate. If it starts at rest, it stays put (boat walker, two skaters on a rope).

The 6 Systems & Gravity traps

  • Measuring r from the surface instead of the center.
  • Squaring the mass factor (only distance is squared).
  • "No gravity in space." Gravity never switches off. Free fall makes apparent weight zero.
  • Mixing up mass (kg, the same everywhere) and weight (N, depends on g).
  • Thinking the bigger object pulls harder. The forces are a 3rd-law pair, so they're equal.
  • Forgetting a mass in the denominator of xcm because it sits at x = 0.
