A machine to slow down gravity

In 1784, George Atwood couldn't time a falling ball accurately, since things fall too fast for the clocks of his day. So he hung two nearly equal masses over a pulley. The pair accelerates at a tiny fraction of g, slow enough to measure. The "Atwood machine" is still in every AP exam.

Elevator cables

A loaded elevator's steel cables carry more than its weight while it speeds up going up, and less while it slows down. Engineers design for the biggest tension, not the weight.

Stage 3 · Learn · 35–45 min

The ideas, one at a time

A. What tension is

  • A rope can only pull, and always along the rope, away from the object.
  • Ideal (massless) rope: the tension is the same all along it. Cut it anywhere, and each piece pulls the other with T.
  • Ideal pulley (massless, frictionless): it changes the rope's direction but not its tension.
  • Tension isn't "the sum of the pulls". A rope pulled with 500 N at each end has T = 500 N, exactly as if one end were tied to a tree.

B. The two-step method: SYSTEM, then ISOLATE

  1. System (for a): draw a box around all the connected objects. Ropes between them are internal, so T cancels. Use ΣFexternal along the rope = (total mass) × a.
  2. Isolate (for T): pick the simplest single object, draw its own FBD (now T is external to it) and write ΣF = ma.
  3. Check: use the other object to see if you get the same T. Ask whether T sits between the forces it has to fight.

Signs made simple: for each object, choose + as the direction it moves. "Down" for the falling block and "up" for the rising one are both +.

D. Angled ropes in equilibrium

2T sin θ = W → T = W / (2 sin θ)

For a weight hanging from two symmetric ropes at angle θ above the horizontal, only the vertical components hold it up. The flatter the ropes, the bigger the tension, and it heads toward infinity as θ → 0. That's why a clothesline with laundry always sags. It's FRQ Set 3, Q2.

E. Springs Topic 2.8 · check if it's on your test

|Fs| = k |Δx|
  • An ideal spring pushes or pulls back toward its natural length, with a force proportional to the stretch or compression. k (N/m) is its stiffness.
  • A hanging mass at rest: kΔx = mg → Δx = mg/k. Double the mass, double the stretch.
  • A spring scale reads the tension in whatever it's connected to. Two 10 N weights over pulleys on either side of a scale → it reads 10 N, not 20 N.

Case closed: the rope survives

Cut the rope (in your head) in the middle and look at the left half. Team Red pulls it left with 500 N. It isn't accelerating, so the right half must pull it right with 500 N. That pull IS the tension: T = 500 N, safely under 800 N. Team Blue is doing exactly what Priya's tree would do: holding the other end. The coach added two forces that act on opposite ends of the rope. Tension is the force across any cut, not the total of the pulls.

Connected-system points

The "is T reasonable?" justification (TBR part D favourite)

0 / 2

"Yes, my tension matches my diagrams."

2 / 2

"Yes. Block 1 accelerates upward, so its FBD needs T > m₁g. Block 2 accelerates downward, so it needs T < m₂g. My result T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁ + m₂) lies between m₁g and m₂g (for 3 kg and 5 kg: 29 N < 37 N < 49 N), so it's consistent with both diagrams."

FBD rules for connected systems

  • One FBD per object. The weight of the hanging block never appears on the table block's FBD. It reaches the table block only through T.
  • Same rope = same T label on both diagrams, with equal-length arrows.
  • Relative lengths must match the motion: for the block accelerating up, T is longer than mg.
  • A "system" FBD only shows external forces. Leave out the tension between the blocks.

The 6 tension traps

  • Adding the pulls at both ends (tug of war is 500 N, not 1000 N).
  • T = m₂g for a hanging block that's accelerating. It's only true at rest or at constant velocity.
  • Forgetting that every connected mass gets accelerated (all of them go in the denominator).
  • Using one + direction (up) for both blocks of an Atwood, then losing the signs.
  • Pushing with a rope. Ropes only pull.
  • cos instead of sin for the vertical component of an angled rope. Check which angle is given.
